# Conclusion

## 8.1 Summary

This dissertation set out to determine the extent to which kernel-resident tuning, combined with cloud-provided Precision Time Protocol synchronisation, can approach the deterministic tail-latency behaviour of kernel-bypass solutions in a high-frequency trading context. The motivation, developed in Chapters 1 and 2, rests on three converging shifts: tail latency rather than mean latency governs both profitability and regulatory compliance in electronic trading; a carefully tuned Linux kernel now recovers much of the determinism once thought to require kernel bypass; and cloud providers have begun to expose hardware precision time directly to guest instances, supplying for the first time a credible measurement substrate in the public cloud. The review established that no prior study has brought these threads together, quantifying the tail-latency distribution of a cloud-hosted, kernel-tuned system against an analytical bound under an HFT-representative workload with PTP hardware timestamps controlling measurement error.

To address that gap the dissertation proposed a hybrid design that pairs controlled empirical measurement with an analytical scheduling-latency bound. Chapter 4 set out six incremental tuning scenarios on bare-metal cloud instances, a synthetic tick-to-trade workload under Poisson arrivals, and a statistical treatment appropriate to heavy-tailed data. Chapter 5 described the realisation of the benchmark harness, the scripted provisioning of the six scenarios, and a local pilot that validated the measurement pipeline and confirmed the environmental preconditions, in particular the need for the privileged, hardware-timed cloud platform, for the real-time and isolation interventions that distinguish the higher scenarios.

## 8.2 Contributions

The work contributes a hybrid methodology in which observed percentiles are tested against a theoretical prediction rather than reported in isolation; an explicit focus on the cloud-virtualised regime that the existing literature has left uncharacterised; and an open, reproducible benchmark harness, released under a permissive licence, that can serve as a reusable substrate for further low-latency Linux research. Together these establish a framework within which the four research questions can be answered with evidence that is both statistically disciplined and theoretically grounded.

## 8.3 Limitations

Three limitations bound the claims of the study. The workload is synthetic: real exchange feeds exhibit burstiness and protocol-level framing that a Poisson approximation does not capture perfectly. The public-cloud environment introduces a noisy-neighbour variable that placement-group co-location only partially controls, since full isolation would require dedicated-tenancy instances outside the present scope. Finally, the PTP Hardware Clock imposes an irreducible measurement floor of approximately one microsecond, below which effects cannot be distinguished from noise. These limitations constrain the precision of the conclusions rather than their direction, and each is a candidate for the extensions set out below.

## 8.4 Future Work

Three prioritised extensions follow from the design. In the near term, the synthetic feed can be replaced with a replay of publicly archived exchange data, such as a NASDAQ ITCH capture, increasing ecological validity while preserving reproducibility. In the medium term, the analytical bound can be extended to account for virtualisation overhead, building on existing characterisations of interrupt latency under hardware virtualisation. In the longer term, FPGA-accelerated strategy evaluation offers a path to close the remaining gap with dedicated-hardware trading infrastructure without abandoning cloud deployment, combining the flexibility of the kernel-tuned pipeline with hardware acceleration where it is most effective.

## 8.5 Concluding Remarks

Tail-latency determinism in cloud-hosted Linux stands at an inflection point. Kernel-resident optimisation has been shown, across a fragmented empirical literature, to recover much of the advantage previously attributed to kernel bypass; cloud providers have begun exposing precision time-synchronisation primitives once confined to specialised trading infrastructure; and formal scheduling-latency analysis has matured enough for theoretically grounded bounds to be derived for the kernels practitioners actually deploy. By bringing these threads together under an HFT-representative workload with explicit analytical grounding, this dissertation sets out a feasible, ethically unproblematic, and empirically rigorous basis for characterising deterministic latency in modern virtualised systems, yielding both a practitioner-usable tuning recipe and a scientific contribution to the understanding of low-latency behaviour in the cloud.
