# 6 Results and Analysis

The real-measurement study ran for 3.6 hours on 15 October 2026 and completed all 401 planned measurements without interruption. Every number in this chapter was taken from the summaries generated by the analysis scripts of Section 5.3. Latencies are in microseconds; OTHER denotes SCHED_OTHER and FIFO followed by a number denotes SCHED_FIFO at that priority. Full per-configuration tables are in Appendix B.

## 6.1 The Untuned Baseline

The ten baseline measurements of Phase A collected 1,437,824 wake-up samples. Their distribution has a floor and a long tail: the median of every run was 56 microseconds, about 0.27 per cent of wake-ups took longer than one millisecond, the pooled p99.9 was 2,926 microseconds (95 per cent bootstrap interval 2,858 to 3,002), and two samples exceeded the 20-millisecond histogram range. The per-run p99.9 ranged from 2,494 to 3,086 microseconds, a coefficient of variation of about 6 per cent. Re-measured five times during Phase C, about three hours later, the default had a pooled p99.9 of 2,541 microseconds (2,159 to 2,780), about 13 per cent lower, a drift far smaller than the effects below. The one-minute load average had a median of 14.3, above the twelve hardware threads, so the contention was as heavy as designed.

The real-time scheduling class dominates. SCHED_FIFO divided the p99.9 by about 32 and the p99 by between 6 and 27 depending on isolation level, and the separation is complete across the whole study: all 239 SCHED_FIFO measurements had a p99.9 of at most 124 microseconds and all 162 SCHED_OTHER measurements at least 1,616. SCHED_FIFO also removed the 56-microsecond floor, lowering the median wake-up to 5 or 6 microseconds, and its effect did not depend on isolation.

Isolating the core by affinity, on its own, made the tail worse. Under SCHED_OTHER the median p99 rose from 80 to about 480 microseconds, about six-fold; under SCHED_FIFO the p99 rose from 11 to 18; and the p99.9 rose by a factor of 1.34 with an interval that excludes one. This is the opposite of what the proposal assumed and motivated the follow-up of Section 6.4. Isolation did cut the worst case under SCHED_FIFO from 3,835 and 9,388 microseconds to below 1,000, consistent with a real-time thread on a shared core still being delayed by non-preemptible kernel work done for the load, although this rests on few maxima.

Freeing the SMT sibling helped the extreme tail and was the only form of isolation that did. Relative to isolating the core alone it multiplied the p99.9 by 0.55, and the best factorial configuration, SCHED_FIFO on CPU 5 with core and sibling free, reached a pooled p99.9 of 50 microseconds. The p99 behaved differently: under SCHED_OTHER freeing the sibling lowered it from about 480 to about 210 microseconds, but under SCHED_FIFO it raised it from 18 to about 34, three times the value on a shared core. Section 6.4 links this to idle states.

The choice of CPU made no detectable difference, although the interval cannot exclude an effect of up to 25 per cent. The measured CPU handled 5,200 to 6,800 interrupts per second in every configuration, almost all local-timer interrupts; device interrupts were negligible on this idle-network workstation. Pinning itself is assessed observationally from Phases B and C: among SCHED_FIFO measurements the median p99.9 was 23 microseconds unpinned (65 measurements) and 33 pinned on a shared core (92), not a significant difference (two-sided Mann-Whitney U, p = 0.36), although because the searches chose these measurements the comparison is indicative only.

## 6.3 Search on the Real Host (RQ2)

Each method spent 30 measurements per seed, five seeds each, on the 3,700-configuration space. Figure 6.2 shows the best p99.9 observed so far against the number of measurements. In the median over seeds both methods found a configuration with an observed p99.9 below 20 microseconds within about 10 measurements; nine of the ten searches did so. After thirty measurements the median best observed value was 11 microseconds for Bayesian optimisation and 15 for random search. The difference in how the budgets were spent is larger: after its eight random initial points, Bayesian optimisation proposed SCHED_FIFO in 85 per cent of its measurements and the median p99.9 of all its measurements was 54 microseconds, whereas random search proposed SCHED_FIFO in 43 per cent of cases and had a median of 1,820 microseconds. Every one of the ten picks uses SCHED_FIFO and none uses core isolation.

![Figure 6.2: Best observed p99.9 against the number of measurements, median over five seeds with the range across seeds shaded. The dashed line is the median of the untuned default.](figures/real_convergence.png)

The best observed values are biased downwards, which is why Phase C re-measured every pick five times (Table B.3). Figure 6.3 compares the tail of the best re-measured pick of each method with the default. Re-measurement confirmed the picks but not their exact values: the re-measured median p99.9 of the picks was 19 microseconds for Bayesian optimisation and 61 for random search, against best observed medians of 11 and 15. Several pooled intervals are wide, from about 15 to 85 microseconds, because the tails consist of rare bursts that some runs contain and others do not; one re-measurement of the Bayesian-optimisation pick of seed 1 contained the only overflow in any SCHED_FIFO measurement while its p99.9 stayed at 17 microseconds. A p99.9 measured over 30 seconds is thus a reliable ranking statistic between configurations that differ by an order of magnitude, but a noisy one between configurations that differ by a factor of two.

![Figure 6.3: Complementary cumulative distribution of wake-up latency, pooled over the five re-evaluation runs, for the untuned default and for the best re-measured pick of each method. The dotted line marks the 99.9th percentile.](figures/real_tail_ccdf.png)

H1 is supported. Against the default re-measured in the same phase, every one of the ten picks reduced the pooled p99.9 by at least a factor of 37 and the median pick by about 49. The best re-measured pick (SCHED_FIFO at priority 1, unpinned) reached 14 microseconds (12 to 17), a factor of about 180, although choosing the best of ten picks reintroduces some selection effect. Even the highest upper bound of any pick (87 microseconds) against the lower bound of the default gives a factor of about 25, and Cliff's delta is at most minus 0.997 for every pick.

H2 is not refuted. The Vargha-Delaney statistic for the re-measured picks is A12 = 0.78 in favour of Bayesian optimisation, so no disadvantage was observed, but the advantage is not significant with five seeds per method (one-sided Mann-Whitney U, p = 0.09). Under the descriptive criterion of Section 3.3 this is weak evidence; a positive demonstration of non-inferiority would need a pre-specified margin and more seeds.

## 6.4 Follow-up: Why Isolation Hurt

The adverse effect of isolation was not anticipated, so a follow-up of 35 measurements was run after the main study with the same harness and contention. The hypothesis was that an isolated core falls idle between wake-ups and enters processor idle states (C-states), paying their exit latency on every wake-up; this processor offers states from C1 to C10 with declared exit latencies from 2 to 890 microseconds, and without root privileges cyclictest cannot hold the power-management latency target at zero. The test adds a keep-awake process, a busy loop pinned to the measured CPU under SCHED_IDLE, which keeps the core out of idle states but yields at once to any other runnable task, and records how often the CPU entered each idle state. It also checks whether the 56-microsecond floor of SCHED_OTHER is caused by the default 50-microsecond timer slack. Five runs of each of seven conditions were measured in shuffled order on CPU 5 (Table B.2).

The results support the idle-state explanation. When the core was shared with the load it went idle about five times per second; once isolated with its sibling free it entered an idle state more than 6,000 times per second, after almost every wake-up, about 95 per cent of them to the shallow C1E and C3 states. The keep-awake process reduced this to zero and the penalty disappeared: under SCHED_FIFO the p99 returned from 33 to 14 microseconds, the same as on the shared core (12), and under SCHED_OTHER the p99 fell from 272 to 111 and the pooled p99.9 from about 2,180 to about 990. With idle states removed, an isolated core with a free sibling matched a shared core at the median and p99 and had a lower extreme tail (pooled p99.9 of 53 against 93 microseconds under SCHED_FIFO), although with five runs that last difference is not significant, whereas the p99 contrast is clear (33 or 34 microseconds in every run without keep-awake, against 12 to 15 on the shared core). The keep-awake process could also change other per-core behaviour, such as the idle tick, so the experiment supports the explanation without ruling out every alternative, and it tested only the core-and-sibling-free condition. The timer-slack check was negative: the 56-microsecond floor is not caused by timer slack, and its origin was not identified further.

## 6.5 Simulation Study (RQ2 and RQ3)

The synthetic model has an exact optimum of 10.29 microseconds against 766.67 for its default configuration. Figure 6.4 and Table B.4 report the regret of the picks over 30 seeds per budget. At a budget of 10 both methods are random searches and the difference (A12 = 0.61, p = 0.065) reflects only different random draws. From 20 evaluations onwards the difference is large and consistent (A12 of 0.96 or higher, p < 0.001): Bayesian optimisation reaches within five per cent of the optimum in 83 per cent of seeds at 20, in every seed from 40, and finds the exact optimum in 77 per cent of seeds at 150, whereas the median regret of random search is still 4.9 per cent at 150 and only half of its picks are then within five per cent.

![Figure 6.4: Regret of the picks of each method against the exact optimum of the synthetic model, median and interquartile range over 30 seeds.](figures/sim_regret_vs_budget.png)

The sensitivity study completed the base model and 56 of the 88 one-at-a-time perturbations before the run was stopped to free the machine for the real measurements; every coefficient is represented by at least one completed perturbation, and the joint Sobol sweep was not started, so the results describe robustness to changing one assumption at a time. Across the 57 models the optimum ranged from 5.1 to 22.5 microseconds and the default was 34 to 176 times worse than the optimum, so the perturbations changed the problem substantially. The conclusion survived every one of them (Figure 6.5): at every budget of 20 or more Bayesian optimisation was significantly better in every model, with a lowest A12 of 0.87 at a budget of 20 and at least 0.93 at larger budgets. Because all models share the same 30 seeds they are not independent replicates, and the uniform result is best read as stability under each single perturbation rather than as 57 separate confirmations.

![Figure 6.5: Vargha-Delaney A12 of Bayesian optimisation against random search for every completed model, by budget. Values above 0.5 favour Bayesian optimisation; the dashed line marks no difference and the dotted line a large effect (0.71).](figures/sim_sensitivity_a12.png)

The ranking of parameter importance was also stable (Figure 6.6). The number of isolated cores was the most important parameter by exact total-effect Sobol index in all 57 models, nohz_full second in 89 per cent, and real-time priority last in 91 per cent. The SHAP rankings estimated from the random-search trials alone recovered the most and least important parameters in 57 and 56 models respectively but were less certain in the middle of the ranking. The synthetic model makes core isolation its dominant parameter whereas the real study found isolation by affinity harmful on its own; Section 7.1 explains why the two are compatible.
