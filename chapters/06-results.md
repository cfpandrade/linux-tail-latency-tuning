# 6 Results and Analysis

The real-measurement study ran for 3.6 hours on 15 October 2026 and completed all 401 planned measurements. Every number below was taken from the summaries generated by the analysis script of Section 5.2 or recomputed from the raw logs by scripts kept in the repository. Latencies are in microseconds; OTHER denotes SCHED_OTHER and FIFO followed by a number denotes SCHED_FIFO at that priority. Full per-configuration tables are in Appendix B.

## 6.1 The Untuned Baseline

The ten baseline measurements of Phase A collected 1,437,824 wake-up samples. The median of every run was 56 microseconds, about 0.27 per cent of wake-ups took longer than one millisecond, the pooled p99.9 was 2,926 microseconds (95 per cent bootstrap interval 2,858 to 3,002), and two samples exceeded the 20-millisecond histogram range; the per-run p99.9 had a coefficient of variation of about 6 per cent. Re-measured in Phase C about three hours later, the default had a pooled p99.9 of 2,541 microseconds (2,159 to 2,780), a drift far smaller than the effects below. The one-minute load average across the study had a median of 14.3, above the twelve hardware threads.

The real-time scheduling class dominates. SCHED_FIFO divided the p99.9 by about 32 and the p99 by between 6 and 27 depending on isolation level. The separation is complete across the whole study: all 239 SCHED_FIFO measurements had a p99.9 of at most 124 microseconds and all 162 SCHED_OTHER measurements at least 1,616. SCHED_FIFO also removed the 56-microsecond floor, lowering the median wake-up to 5 or 6 microseconds.

Isolating the core by affinity, on its own, made the tail worse. Under SCHED_OTHER the median p99 rose from about 80 to about 480 microseconds, about six-fold; under SCHED_FIFO from 11 to 18; and the p99.9 rose by a factor of 1.34 with an interval that excludes one. This is the opposite of what the proposal assumed and motivated the follow-up of Section 6.4. Isolation did cut the worst case under SCHED_FIFO from 3,835 and 9,388 microseconds to below 1,000.

Freeing the SMT sibling helped the extreme tail and was the only form of isolation that did: relative to isolating the core alone it multiplied the p99.9 by 0.55, and the best factorial configuration, SCHED_FIFO on CPU 5 with core and sibling free, reached a pooled p99.9 of 50 microseconds. The p99 behaved differently. Under SCHED_OTHER freeing the sibling lowered it from about 480 to about 210 microseconds, but under SCHED_FIFO it raised it from 18 to about 34, which Section 6.4 links to idle states.

The choice of CPU made no detectable difference: the measured CPU handled 5,200 to 6,800 interrupts per second in every configuration, almost all local-timer interrupts, and device interrupts were negligible on this idle-network workstation. Pinning itself, assessed observationally from Phases B and C, made no significant difference either (median p99.9 of 23 microseconds unpinned against 33 pinned on a shared core among SCHED_FIFO measurements; two-sided Mann-Whitney U, p = 0.36), although as the searches chose these measurements the comparison is indicative only.

## 6.3 Search on the Real Host (RQ2)

Each method spent 30 measurements per seed, five seeds each, on the 3,700-configuration space (Figure 6.2). In the median over seeds both methods found a configuration with an observed p99.9 below 20 microseconds within about 10 measurements, and after 30 the median best observed value was 11 microseconds for Bayesian optimisation and 15 for random search. The difference in how the budgets were spent is larger. Over measurements 9 to 30 of every seed, after the eight random initial points of Bayesian optimisation, it proposed SCHED_FIFO in 85 per cent of its measurements and the median p99.9 of all its measurements was 54 microseconds, whereas random search over the same measurements proposed SCHED_FIFO in 43 per cent of cases and had a median of 1,820 microseconds. Every one of the ten picks uses SCHED_FIFO and none uses core isolation.

![Figure 6.2: Best observed p99.9 against the number of measurements, median over five seeds with the range across seeds shaded. The dashed line is the median per-run p99.9 of the untuned default in Phase A.](figures/real_convergence.png)

The best observed values are biased downwards, which is why Phase C re-measured every pick five times (Table B.3); Figure 6.3 compares the tail of the best re-measured pick of each method with the default. Re-measurement confirmed the picks but not their values: the re-measured median p99.9 of the picks was 19 microseconds for Bayesian optimisation and 61 for random search, against best observed medians of 11 and 15, and several pooled intervals span 15 to 85 microseconds because the tails consist of rare bursts that only some runs contain. A 30-second p99.9 thus ranks configurations reliably when they differ by an order of magnitude, but not when they differ by a factor of two.

![Figure 6.3: Complementary cumulative distribution of wake-up latency, pooled over the five re-evaluation runs, for the untuned default and for the best re-measured pick of each method. The dotted line marks the 99.9th percentile.](figures/real_tail_ccdf.png)

H1 is supported. Against the default re-measured in the same phase, every one of the ten picks reduced the pooled p99.9 by a factor of at least about 37 and the median pick by about 49; the best re-measured pick (SCHED_FIFO at priority 1, unpinned) reached 14 microseconds (12 to 17), although choosing the best of ten picks reintroduces some selection effect. Cliff's delta is at most minus 0.997 for every pick: almost every wake-up of a tuned configuration was faster than almost every wake-up of the default.

H2 is not refuted. The Vargha-Delaney statistic for the re-measured picks is A12 = 0.78 in favour of Bayesian optimisation, so no disadvantage was observed, but the advantage is not significant with five seeds per method (one-sided Mann-Whitney U, p = 0.09). Under the descriptive criterion of Section 3.3 this is weak evidence.

## 6.4 Follow-up: Why Isolation Hurt

The adverse effect of isolation was not anticipated, so a follow-up of 35 measurements was run after the main study with the same harness and contention. The hypothesis was that an isolated core falls idle between wake-ups, enters processor idle states (C-states), and pays their exit latency on every wake-up, which the kernel's cpuidle interface reports as 2 to 890 microseconds for this processor; without root cyclictest cannot hold the power-management latency target at zero. The test adds a keep-awake process, a busy loop pinned to the measured CPU under SCHED_IDLE, which keeps the core out of idle states but yields at once to any other runnable task, and records how often the CPU entered each idle state. Five runs of each of seven conditions were measured in shuffled order on CPU 5 (Table B.2).

The results support the explanation. A core shared with the load went idle about five times per second; isolated with its sibling free, it entered an idle state more than 6,000 times per second, after almost every wake-up, mostly to the shallow C1E and C3 states. The keep-awake process reduced this to zero and the penalty disappeared: under SCHED_FIFO the p99 returned from 33 to 14 microseconds, close to the 12 of the shared core, and under SCHED_OTHER the p99 fell from 272 to 111 and the pooled p99.9 from about 2,180 to about 990. With idle states removed, an isolated core with a free sibling matched a shared core at the median and p99 and had a lower extreme tail (pooled p99.9 of 53 against 93 microseconds under SCHED_FIFO), although with five runs that difference is not significant. The keep-awake process could also change other per-core behaviour, such as the idle tick, so the experiment supports the explanation without ruling out every alternative, and it tested only the core-and-sibling-free condition. A check of timer slack as the cause of the 56-microsecond floor of SCHED_OTHER was negative.

## 6.5 Simulation Study (RQ2 and RQ3)

The synthetic model has an exact optimum of 10.29 microseconds against 766.67 for its default configuration. Figure 6.4 and Table B.4 report the regret of the picks over 30 seeds per budget. At a budget of 10 both methods are random searches and the difference (A12 = 0.61, p = 0.065) reflects only different random draws. From 20 evaluations onwards the difference is large and consistent (A12 of 0.96 or higher, p < 0.001): Bayesian optimisation reaches within 5 per cent of the optimum in 83 per cent of seeds at 20, in every seed from 40, and finds the exact optimum in 77 per cent of seeds at 150, whereas the median regret of random search is still 4.9 per cent at 150.

![Figure 6.4: Regret of the picks of each method against the exact optimum of the synthetic model, median and interquartile range over 30 seeds. The dotted line marks a regret of 5 per cent, the threshold used in the text and in Table B.4.](figures/sim_regret_vs_budget.png)

The sensitivity study completed the base model and 56 of the 88 one-at-a-time perturbations before the run was stopped to free the machine for the real measurements; every coefficient is represented by at least one completed perturbation, and the joint Sobol sweep was not started, so the results describe robustness to changing one assumption at a time. Across the 57 models the optimum ranged from 5.1 to 22.5 microseconds, so the perturbations changed the problem substantially. The conclusion survived every one of them (Figure 6.5): at every budget of 20 or more Bayesian optimisation was significantly better in every model, with a lowest A12 of 0.87. Because all models share the same 30 seeds they are not independent replicates, so the result is best read as stability under each single perturbation rather than as 57 separate confirmations.

![Figure 6.5: Vargha-Delaney A12 of Bayesian optimisation against random search for every completed model, by budget. Points are the 56 one-at-a-time perturbations, boxes their quartiles, and the diamond the unperturbed base model. Values above 0.5 favour Bayesian optimisation; the dashed line marks no difference and the dotted line a large effect (0.71).](figures/sim_sensitivity_a12.png)

The ranking of parameter importance was also stable (Figure 6.6): the number of isolated cores was the most important parameter by exact total-effect Sobol index in all 57 models, nohz_full second in 89 per cent, and real-time priority last in 91 per cent, and SHAP rankings estimated from the random-search trials alone ranked isolated cores first in all 57 models and real-time priority last in 56, agreeing with the exact ranking of the least important parameter in 51. The synthetic model makes core isolation its dominant parameter whereas the real study found isolation by affinity harmful on its own; Section 7.1 explains why the two are compatible.
